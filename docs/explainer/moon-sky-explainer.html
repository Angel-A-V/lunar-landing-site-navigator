Where are the Sun and Earth in the sky of a spot on the Moon?

That is the whole project. Pick a landing site and a time. Answer with two directions: where the Sun is and where Earth is, measured from the ground there. Sun height tells you solar power. Earth above the horizon tells you whether you can radio home directly.

One correction to the mental picture

You are not an observer on Earth looking at the Moon. The observer stands on the Moon, at the landing site. Earth and the Sun are the things being looked at. The frames you described are the right ones. The chain just runs from space, to the Moon, to the site.

The frame chain

Three coordinate frames, each one re-describes the same arrows

Frame 1 · Space (inertial)

Axes never rotate. Everything moves. This is where an ephemeris (JPL Horizons) gives positions of Sun, Earth and Moon.

→rotate by the Moon's orientation
(quaternion or matrix, includes libration)
Frame 2 · Moon-fixed

The Moon is held still. Sun and Earth become directions that sweep around it. A site is just a latitude and longitude on this ball.

→project onto the site's own east / north / up
Frame 3 · Site-local (az / el)

What a person on the ground sees. Azimuth is the compass direction, elevation is height above the horizon. This is the only frame that answers power and radio questions.

The step from frame 2 to 3 is one dot product per axis: elevation = asin(direction · up), azimuth = atan2(direction · east, direction · north). Sun and Earth go through exactly the same step. The model below does that math live.

Teaching model: Sun path uses a constant Sun latitude you choose (±1.54° is the Moon's axial tilt, [verify]) and a 29.53-day lunar day. Earth uses a simple libration swing of about ±6.7° latitude and ±7.9° longitude. There is no terrain, so mountains and crater rims are not blocking anything. Site coordinates are approximate. The real app replaces this with JPL Horizons data and LOLA horizon profiles.
